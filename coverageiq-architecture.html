How the rule library grows

Not a model-training problem — an operating model. A forward-deployed team embeds with each customer's own coverage specialists, and the rules get better because the people who know the risk best helped write them.

Recommendation: not now

Do we need to train our own model?

No — not for the reasoning itself, and not yet for anything else. This domain runs on judgment calls that shift by policy, by carrier, by jurisdiction: when a missing declarations page should stop a rule cold versus when the wording already answers the question on its own. That kind of nuance is exactly what a large general-purpose model, given a well-written skill, is good at — and exactly what a fine-tuned model would bake in at training time, making it slow and expensive to correct.

The evidence for this is in the build itself, not a hypothesis: a real inconsistency was found this week in how one rule handled two different incomplete policy wordings. The fix — rewriting the rule's guidance and adding a general principle for every future rule to inherit — took minutes to write and was validated the same day against both real documents. A fine-tuning cycle to correct the same behavior would have taken a training run, a new eval pass, and a redeploy.

Where a smaller or custom model could genuinely help later, as a cost and latency optimization once volume and rule patterns are stable: a cheap triage classifier ahead of the full skill chain (a fast first pass at coverage classification before the expensive reasoning model runs), or fine-tuning the highest-volume, most literal rules once their pattern has been proven stable across many real policies. Both are optimizations to revisit after the rule library is mature — not requirements to get started.
