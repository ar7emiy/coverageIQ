How do we make CoverageIQ reliable as it scales?

Not by trusting the model more as volume grows — by keeping its job narrow, and putting people in every place that actually requires judgment.

We're not asking AI to determine what good coverage looks like. We're using AI to consistently apply standards that SMEs already defined.

Speaker notes

Open here. This is the objection everyone in the room is quietly holding — "how do we know the AI isn't just making up what good coverage looks like?" Name it directly, then let the diagram answer it before anyone has to ask.

Speaker notes

Walk left to right. SMEs already know what good coverage looks like — that expertise exists in the room today, nothing new required of them conceptually. FDAs are the new role: they translate that judgment into something precise enough to test. The AI box is drawn as the narrowest one on purpose — it doesn't get an opinion, it applies what's already been decided. Point out that Legal & Compliance sits downstream of AI, not upstream — they define the boundary before anything ships and audit it after. Platform & Engineering is the one box that's never "done," which is why it's drawn underneath everything else instead of in the sequence.

What FDA + SME collaboration actually looks like

A sample four-week cadence for taking one rule from nothing to production — one FDA, one SME, working sessions together, not a document handed back and forth.

Week 1 2 3 4
WEEK 1

Shadow & learn

  • FDA sits in on the SME's actual policy reviews — real ones, not hypotheticals
  • FDA asks "why" at every judgment call: why this counts, why that doesn't
  • Output: a working list of edge cases, in the SME's own words
WEEK 2

Draft together

  • FDA writes the first draft of the rule; SME reviews it line by line, live
  • Corrections happen in the room, not as a redline emailed back a week later
  • Output: a rule draft, plus a signed-off edge-case list
WEEK 3

Test against reality

  • The draft rule runs against a fresh batch of that team's real, unseen policies
  • SME checks every result against what a person would have concluded
  • Output: a scorecard against real cases, not synthetic ones
WEEK 4

Sign off & deploy

  • SME formally signs off that the rule reflects their standard — not the FDA
  • Rule merges into the shared, versioned library — live for every team from that point on
  • Output: one more reusable rule, and a working rhythm for the next one

Illustrative cadence for one rule on one coverage line. In practice an FDA and SME team often run several rules in parallel once they've found their rhythm — the four-week arc compresses as trust and shared vocabulary build up.

Speaker notes

This is the part executives usually can't picture — make it concrete. It's a four-week rhythm per rule, working sessions between one FDA and one SME, not a hand-off of documents. Emphasize week 3: the rule gets tested against that team's own real, unseen policies before anyone signs off — nothing goes live on a hunch. And the SME signs off, not the FDA — the domain expert keeps the actual authority the whole way through.

What already makes this reliable, today

Not promises for the roadmap — mechanisms already built into the pilot.

Traceable

Evidence-cited, always

Every finding traces to the exact policy quote and page it came from. No black-box scores, nothing asserted without a citation.

Honest

MANUAL_REVIEW is a real outcome

When a rule genuinely can't tell, it says so and routes to a person — it isn't forced into PASS or a false positive to look decisive.

Tested

Every rule change is validated

Rule edits are tested against real policies before they reach the shared library — the same rigor as this build, every time.

Auditable

Full audit trail

Every result records which rule version produced it and why — reviewable by Legal & Compliance at any time, not just at launch.

Speaker notes

These four aren't promises for the roadmap — they're already true of the pilot the panel has seen output from. If someone asks "how do we know it won't get less careful as it scales," this is the answer: none of these four mechanisms is volume-dependent.

From POC to production

Three phases over twelve months — each one building on validated work from the last, not a rewrite. Same rigor that produced this pilot, applied to more coverage lines and more teams.

Month 0 3 6 12
0 – 3 MONTHS

Productionize the core rule set

  • Bring D&O, EPLI, Crime, and Fiduciary Liability rules to production quality
  • Validate rules against a real library of customer policies, not just pilot samples
  • Security review and production architecture build-out — Azure environment, identity, data handling
3 – 6 MONTHS

MVP deployment

  • Initial adoption — first live customer teams on the production system
  • Change management and internal marketing to drive real usage, not just access
  • Focus group studies with broker and underwriting teams to refine the experience
  • Continued FDA work validating and refining rules against live usage
6 – 12 MONTHS

Rule library expansion

  • New specialty coverage lines and customer teams onboarded
  • Additional FDA engagements scale the operating model — Engage → Learn → Co-author → Test → Deploy
  • The rule library compounds: each new engagement builds on what the last one already validated
Speaker notes

Emphasize continuity between phases — this isn't three separate projects, it's one team compounding its own work. Phase 1 has no customer-facing surface yet — it's earning the right to be trusted with real usage in phase 2. If asked "why not launch faster," the answer is in phase 1's own list: security review and rule validation aren't corners we're allowed to cut on an insurance product.
